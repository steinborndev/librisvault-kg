# Plasma complement dysregulation in Down syndrome with and without dementia

A cross-sectional biomarker study of the complement system in adults with [[Down syndrome]]. Down syndrome brings immune dysregulation and near-universal early [[Alzheimer disease]] pathology, and complement is a driver of neuroinflammation in Alzheimer disease, yet complement had never been profiled comprehensively in Down syndrome plasma. The authors measured 14 complement proteins and asked three questions: is complement abnormal in Down syndrome, does it differ with dementia, and do Alzheimer risk genotypes ([[APOE]], [[CLU]], [[CR1]]) move complement levels.

## Design

- 71 adults with Down syndrome from the [[LonDownS cohort]] (trisomy confirmed genetically; 13 with clinically diagnosed dementia, mean age 54 versus 38 in the non-demented, p < 0.0001) and 46 age-matched healthy controls from Newcastle. Neither group had extra freeze-thaw cycles; Down syndrome and control samples were run on separate plates.
- In-house and commercial sandwich ELISAs for 14 analytes: components [[C1QA]] (C1q), [[C3]], C4 and [[C9]]; regulators [[SERPING1]] (C1 inhibitor), [[CFI]] (factor I), soluble [[CR1]] (sCR1), [[CFH]] (factor H), [[CFHR4]], FHR125 and [[CLU]] (clusterin); activation products iC3b, C5a and the [[Membrane attack complex]] (terminal complement complex, TCC). Activation products were assayed immediately after thawing. Intra-assay CV was under 10% and inter-assay CV 1 to 14%.
- Genotyping (Illumina OmniExpressExome array for SNPs, TaqMan for the APOE alleles) was available for 58 participants with Down syndrome (63 for APOE). Tested SNPs: [[CR1 rs6656401]], [[CR1 rs6691117]] and [[CLU rs11136000]]; APOE ε4 carriers (n = 16, 2 with dementia).
- Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis, t-tests and Pearson correlation; no adjustment for age or sex because no correlations with protein levels were found.

## Findings

- Down syndrome versus controls: higher C1q (mean 191 versus 110 μg/ml), C3 and C9 (p < 0.05); higher activation products TCC and iC3b (p < 0.001); higher C1 inhibitor (150 versus 118 μg/ml), clusterin (292 versus 172 μg/ml), factor H and FHR4 (p < 0.001); lower factor I and sCR1 (p < 0.01). C4, C5a and FHR125 did not differ. A subset of Down syndrome participants had very high TCC and C5a.
- The authors read the regulator pattern as dysregulation mainly of the alternative pathway convertases, with elevated classical and terminal pathway markers, likely reflecting generalised immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation (autoimmune comorbidity), not a specific Alzheimer effect.
- Dementia within Down syndrome: C3 (p < 0.01) and factor I (p < 0.05) were lower in the 13 with dementia; clusterin was lower and C1q higher without reaching significance. The authors discuss that low plasma C3 could reflect consumption by Alzheimer pathology (C3-deficient APP transgenic mice had more Aβ at 17 months) and that low factor I is also linked to [[Age-related macular degeneration]].
- Within Down syndrome, plasma C3 correlated with BMI (r = 0.26, p < 0.05), tying to [[Obesity]]-driven inflammation; FHR4 was higher and TCC lower in women.
- Genotype: CR1 rs6656401 minor-allele (A) carriers had higher sCR1 than GG homozygotes (14.6 versus 11.8 ng/ml, p < 0.05), replicating a finding from the general population even though sCR1 was lower overall in Down syndrome. rs6691117 did not change sCR1 and rs11136000 did not change clusterin. APOE ε4 carriers did not differ from non-carriers in any complement protein.

## Why This Source Matters

It gives the first broad plasma complement profile in adults with Down syndrome and shows that most of the abnormality is shared by the whole Down syndrome group, so complement markers by themselves signal immune dysregulation rather than dementia. The two measures that separate Down syndrome with dementia from those without (lower C3 and factor I) are candidate additions to Alzheimer-specific biomarkers for early diagnosis in this high-risk population. It also extends the CR1 sCR1 genotype effect to a trisomy 21 population.

## Assessment

- Small dementia group (13), cross-sectional, and the dementia group was about 16 years older, so age is confounded with dementia although the authors report no age correlation with the proteins. The dementia comparison is written as n = 58 non-demented in the abstract and 57 in a figure legend.
- Controls and Down syndrome samples came from different sites and were run on separate ELISA plates; BMI was unavailable for controls; dementia was taken from clinical records, not a standard diagnostic battery.
- Only 16 APOE ε4 carriers, so the null APOE result has low power; the SNP analyses used 58 genotyped participants. Multiple testing across 14 analytes is not described as corrected.
- The text states the SNP once as rs669117; the figure legend and discussion give rs6691117.

## Open Questions

- Do plasma C3 and factor I fall before clinical dementia in Down syndrome (cross-sectional design; longitudinal LonDownS follow-up not analysed)?
- Why does a subset of people with Down syndrome have very high TCC and C5a (autoimmune comorbidity suggested but not tested)?
- Is plasma clusterin a useful marker in early-onset, genetically determined Alzheimer disease, given no difference was seen between demented and non-demented Down syndrome (no published familial Alzheimer cohort data cited)?
