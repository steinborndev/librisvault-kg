# Ajugol and isoacteoside as anti-Alzheimer components of Rehmannia root

A preclinical study of why the raw root of *Rehmannia glutinosa* and its rice-wine-steamed form, both traditional remedies for dementia, might help in [[Alzheimer disease]], and which single compound in each carries the effect. It combines compound profiling in an amyloid mouse model with network pharmacology, proteomics, gut microbiome sequencing, docking, and validation in a chemically induced mouse model and in microglial cells. Root preparations are described on [[Rehmanniae radix and Rehmanniae radix praeparata]].

## Why This Source Matters

It is a worked example of reducing a multi-component herbal preparation to two marker compounds with distinct proposed mechanisms: [[Ajugol]] from the raw root toward the inflammation and apoptosis axes, [[Isoacteoside]] from the processed root toward autophagy. The mechanistic claims rest on protein-level readouts in one non-transgenic mouse model and one microglial line, so they are leads rather than established targets (see Assessment).

## Design

- Compound profiling: aqueous extracts of raw root (RR) and processed root (RRP) were analysed by UPLC with Q-Exactive tandem mass spectrometry (26 peaks in RR, 31 in RRP). Serum and brain of 9-month-old [[APP-PS1 mouse]] males (n = 10 per group, 15 g/kg/day by gavage for 4 weeks, wild-type C57BL/6J controls) were screened, keeping compounds whose intensity rose more than threefold after dosing.
- Network pharmacology and docking: targets from TCMSP, TCMID and CTD, an interaction network from STRING, enrichment of the top 20 KEGG pathways, and AutoDock Vina docking of ajugol and isoacteoside against [[TLR4]], [[NFKBIA]], [[NLRP3]], [[BCL2]], [[CASP3]], [[SQSTM1]] and [[MTOR]]. See [[Network pharmacology with multi-omics for herbal medicines]].
- Omics: 16S rRNA sequencing of the faecal microbiota (n = 3 per group) and quantitative brain proteomics (n = 3 per group).
- Validation in vivo: the [[D-galactose and aluminium chloride mouse model]] in ICR mice (n = 10 per group), with donepezil (0.65 mg/kg/day), ajugol (100 or 50 mg/kg/day), isoacteoside (5 or 2.5 mg/kg/day), RR and RRP (15 or 5 g/kg/day) for 4 weeks after 4 weeks of induction. Readouts were Morris water maze, open-field test, thioflavin-S plaque staining, imaging mass spectrometry of hippocampus and western blots.
- Validation in vitro: amyloid-beta 1-42 (10 μM, 24 h) challenge of [[BV2]] microglia, treated with ajugol (50 or 25 μM) or isoacteoside (40 or 20 μM); cytokine ELISA, western blot and immunofluorescence.
- Statistics: t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's test; P < 0.05.

## Findings

- Ajugol was found in serum and brain of mice given either preparation; isoacteoside appeared only after the processed root. Of the RR and RRP compounds in serum, several were shared and several specific to each; the RRP compound network was more complex than the RR one.
- Docking: ajugol bound NF-κB with -7.799 kcal/mol; isoacteoside bound [[TLR4]], [[NFKBIA]] and [[NLRP3]] with -9.668, -9.031 and -9.668 kcal/mol. Pathway enrichment pointed RR to neuronal apoptosis, oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, and RRP to oxidative stress, immune inflammation, nutrient metabolism and cell growth.
- Gut microbiota: 4,949 operational taxonomic units, 319 shared by all four groups. Both preparations raised diversity indices and abundance of *Akkermansia*; the only individually significant genus change was a fall in *Alistipes*. Strongest correlations were ajugol with *Eubacterium* and isoacteoside with *Robinsoniella* and *Bifidobacterium*.
- Proteomics: 5,213 proteins identified (5,169 quantifiable); RR changed 308 (112 up, 196 down) and RRP 51 (24 up, 27 down), enriched for neurodegeneration, [[Neuroinflammation]], [[Autophagy]] and [[Apoptosis]] pathways.
- ICR mice: every treatment group had shorter escape latency, more platform crossings, more time in the target quadrant, less time in the corner and more in the centre of the open field, and smaller thioflavin-S plaque area than model mice. Imaging mass spectrometry found isoacteoside but not ajugol in hippocampus.
- Protein readouts in mouse hippocampus and BV2 cells: [[TLR4]], phosphorylated [[RELA]] and [[NLRP3]] and [[CASP1]] fell and phosphorylated [[NFKBIA]] rose; [[CYCS]] and [[CASP3]] fell while [[BCL2]] and [[BAX]] rose; [[MTOR]] and p-MTOR fell, [[MAP1LC3B]] (LC3-II) fell non-significantly in mice and [[SQSTM1]] rose. Ajugol had the larger effect on the first two sets and isoacteoside on the autophagy set.
- BV2 cytokines: [[IL1B]] fell in all treated groups while [[IL4]], nitric oxide and [[CSF3]] rose; [[IFNG]] differed between compounds. The authors read ajugol as anti-inflammatory and isoacteoside as boosting neurotrophic factors.

## Assessment

- The ICR model is a toxin and sugar-induced aging model, not a genetic Alzheimer model, so its thioflavin-S "plaque" reading and cognitive deficits are a weaker stand-in for amyloid pathology than the transgenic mice used for profiling; the transgenic mice were not used for the behavioural or western blot validation.
- Mechanism rests on correlative protein levels and docking; no knockdown, inhibitor or target-engagement experiment ties either compound to a single pathway, and "ajugol acts on inflammation and apoptosis, isoacteoside on autophagy" is a difference in degree between groups, not exclusivity.
- Ajugol was not detected in hippocampus by imaging mass spectrometry, which the authors attribute to possible metabolism; its direct brain action is therefore unproven.
- Some readouts sit awkwardly with the stated direction: [[BAX]] and nitric oxide rose with treatment, and the text describes isoacteoside as present in serum in one section and only in brain in another.
- Microbiome and proteomic groups had three animals each, and the gut-brain link is correlational (the authors name faecal transplantation as a future test). The dose of 15 g/kg/day corresponds to about 116 g of raw material per 70 kg human.

## Open Questions

- Does ajugol reach the brain in a form that engages the TLR4, NF-κB and NLRP3 axis directly (it was not seen in hippocampus by imaging mass spectrometry; metabolites were not identified)?
- Do ajugol and isoacteoside reproduce the behavioural and plaque benefits in a transgenic amyloid model such as APP/PS1 mice (the validation used a D-galactose and aluminium chloride model)?
- Is the autophagy effect of isoacteoside mediated by inhibition of mTOR itself, given LC3-II fell rather than rose in treated mice?
- Is the microbiome shift under the two root preparations causal for the cognitive effect (only correlations were analysed; faecal microbiota transplantation was proposed but not done)?
- Does either compound change microglial M1/M2 polarisation in amyloid-beta-challenged cells (not examined)?
