# Berberine rescues D-ribose-induced Alzheimer pathology via mitophagy

A preclinical study (International Journal of Molecular Sciences) in [[APP-PS1 mouse]] animals and [[Neuro2a]] cells. It asks whether the sugar [[D-ribose]], which raises amyloid and Tau pathology, damages [[Mitophagy]] through the [[PINK1]]-[[PRKN]] pathway, and whether the alkaloid [[Berberine]] reverses this by lowering methylation of the PINK1 promoter.

## Why This Source Matters

D-ribose is higher in the blood of Alzheimer patients than in cognitively intact people of similar age and is suggested as a biomarker, yet how it harms cognition was unclear. This paper links it to loss of mitophagy and to an epigenetic mechanism (hypermethylation of the PINK1 promoter with higher [[DNMT1]]), and shows a natural product the authors note has been used clinically for many years reversing the memory and pathology changes, though not the anxiety-like behaviour. The mechanistic chain rests on correlation between promoter methylation and expression plus pharmacological and siRNA tests; the numerical effect sizes and group sizes are not in the text read (see Assessment).

## Design

- Groups: control (Ctrl); D-ribose (D); D-ribose plus berberine (DB); D-ribose plus berberine plus the mitophagy inhibitor [[Mdivi-1]] (DBM); D-ribose plus berberine plus PINK1 siRNA (DBS, cells); D-ribose plus the DNA methylation inhibitor [[Azacitidine]] (DA). Doses were given in a table not included in the text.
- Models: [[APP-PS1 mouse]] animals (hippocampus and cortex) and [[Neuro2a]] cells.
- Mitochondrial function: JC-1 membrane potential, DCFH-DA reactive oxygen species and Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis by flow cytometry; cytochrome c release by Western blot ([[CYCS]]).
- Mitophagy: transmission electron microscopy, Mito-Tracker/Lyso-Tracker co-localization, immunofluorescence for PINK1-Parkin and for LC3A/B with [[TOMM20]], Western blot for PINK1, Parkin, [[SQSTM1]] and [[MAP1LC3B]] in isolated mitochondria and cytoplasm.
- Pathology: Western blot and immunohistochemistry for amyloid-beta and phospho-Tau at T205, T231 and S396; H&E and Nissl staining of cortex and hippocampal CA1, CA3 and dentate gyrus.
- Behaviour: [[Morris water maze]], [[Two-way shuttle avoidance task]] and [[Open field test]].
- Epigenetics: promoter methylation of PINK1 by [[Bisulfite sequencing PCR]] in hippocampus, with DNMT1 and PINK1 by Western blot. Statistics were one-way ANOVA with Tukey's test, p below 0.05.

## Findings

- D-ribose lowered mitochondrial membrane potential, raised reactive oxygen species and cytochrome c release and increased apoptosis in N2a cells ([[Mitochondrial dysfunction]]); berberine reversed all of these, and adding Mdivi-1 or PINK1 siRNA removed the rescue.
- By electron microscopy hippocampal mitochondria were swollen with ruptured cristae in the D and DBM groups, but normal with autophagic vesicles in Ctrl and DB. Berberine restored mitochondria-lysosome co-localization and the PINK1-Parkin and LC3A/B-TOMM20 co-localization that D-ribose had reduced.
- D-ribose lowered PINK1, Parkin and LC3B protein in cells and mice; berberine raised them and cleared p62 (SQSTM1), read as activation of PINK1-Parkin mitophagy.
- In APP/PS1 hippocampus and cortex D-ribose raised amyloid-beta and phospho-Tau (T205, T231, S396) ([[Tau hyperphosphorylation]]); berberine lowered them, and Mdivi-1 raised them again. D-ribose also disordered neurons, with pyknotic nuclei, vacuolization and uneven Nissl bodies, which berberine improved ([[Neuronal loss]]).
- Behaviour: D-ribose lengthened water-maze escape latency, shortened target-quadrant time and reduced shuttle number in the avoidance task; berberine reversed these, Mdivi-1 undid the reversal, and swimming speed did not differ. Anxiety-like behaviour in the open field (more time in the periphery) was induced by D-ribose and was not changed by berberine or Mdivi-1 ([[Anxiety]]).
- Epigenetics: PINK1 promoter methylation rose with D-ribose and fell with berberine, to the same extent as with azacitidine; DNMT1 rose and PINK1 fell with D-ribose, and the reverse in the DB and DA groups, in cells and mice ([[DNA methylation]]).

## Assessment

- The text gives no effect sizes, animal numbers, ages, sex, doses or treatment durations (the dose table is not in the text), so the strength of each rescue cannot be judged.
- APP/PS1 mice already carry amyloid pathology, and the design has no wild-type group and no berberine-alone group, so "D-ribose-induced" pathology is an increment on a transgenic background and berberine's own effect is not separated.
- The methylation mechanism is correlative: azacitidine mimics berberine, but the paper does not test whether PINK1 methylation itself (for example by targeted demethylation) is needed for the rescue, and it does not show how berberine reaches DNMT1. Bisulfite primers are given, but the CpG positions analysed are not described.
- PINK1 siRNA and Mdivi-1 were applied to cells (DBS) and animals (DBM) respectively as far as the text shows; Mdivi-1 is treated as a specific mitophagy inhibitor without discussion of other targets.
- Anxiety-like behaviour was not rescued, which the authors note without explanation; the discussion contains garbled sentences about membrane potential and ROS direction.
- The proposal that D-ribose acts through glycation and AGE-RAGE signalling is from earlier work and was not measured here.

## Open Questions

- Does targeted demethylation of the PINK1 promoter, or PINK1 re-expression, reproduce the berberine rescue in D-ribose-treated APP/PS1 mice (only correlation with azacitidine was shown)?
- By what route does berberine lower DNMT1 and PINK1 promoter methylation (not addressed in the paper)?
- Does D-ribose worsen cognition and mitophagy in wild-type mice without transgenic amyloid (no wild-type group was included)?
- Why does berberine fail to relieve D-ribose-induced anxiety-like behaviour while rescuing memory (no explanation given, and the anxiety circuit was not examined)?
- Do circulating D-ribose levels in Alzheimer patients track promoter methylation of PINK1 in blood or brain (the paper cites higher D-ribose in patients but measured no human samples)?
