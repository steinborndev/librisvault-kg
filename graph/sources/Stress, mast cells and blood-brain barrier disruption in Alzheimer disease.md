# Stress, mast cells and blood-brain barrier disruption in Alzheimer disease

This mini-review argues that chronic and acute [[Psychological stress]] can speed up [[Alzheimer disease]] by way of [[Mast cell activation]]. The proposed chain runs from stress, through release of [[CRH]] and activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, to mast cell mediators that inflame the brain and loosen the [[Blood-brain barrier dysfunction|blood-brain barrier]], and from there to more amyloid and tau pathology. It is a narrative review, not a primary study, and it states plainly that the molecular details in humans are not yet known.

## Scope and structure

- Mast cells, pain and neuroinflammation: how mast cell mediators reach nociceptors and glia.
- Stress, pain and neuroinflammation: CRH signalling, dural mast cells and headache.
- Stress effects on the barrier: neurovascular unit, gliovascular unit, tight and adherens junctions.
- Stress and Alzheimer disease: amyloid, tau, cortisol, BDNF, barrier transport of Aβ and tight junction changes.

## Main claims

**Mast cells and pain.**
- Mast cells sit at nerve endings, in the meninges and along brain vessels, and release [[Histamine]], tryptase ([[TPSAB1]]), prostaglandins, [[NGF]], sphingosine-1-phosphate and [[TNF]]. Histamine acts through H1 to H4 receptors; tryptase acts through PAR-2 on nerve endings and raises release of substance P and CGRP, which in turn activate mast cells again; NGF acts through TrkA on mast cells and nerves.
- The review states that about 50% of histamine and 25% of TNF-α in the rat brain come from mast cells, and that even a few mast cells can sustain neuroinflammation because mast cells and glia activate one another in a positive-feedback loop.
- Between 38% and 75% of people with Alzheimer disease and 40% to 86% of those with [[Parkinson disease]] are said to have painful symptoms, often under-treated because advanced dementia limits reporting. See [[Pain]].

**Stress, CRH and mast cells.**
- [[CRH]] is made in neocortex, basal ganglia, amygdala and hippocampus; mast cells carry CRH-R1 and CRH-R2, release CRH themselves, and in turn activate glia. CRH receptor signalling is said to kill neurons through PKA, PKC, calcium and [[NF-kappaB signaling]], to cause spine loss, and to raise barrier permeability directly.
- In C57BL/6 mice acute immobilization stress activated dural mast cells, but not in neurokinin-1 receptor knockout mice, and stress-induced vascular leak was reduced in mast cell deficient mice. Stress worsens [[Migraine]] and headache through dural mast cells.

**Barrier and junction changes.**
- In rats, restraint stress lowered [[CLDN5]] (claudin-5) and [[OCLN]] (occludin) in hippocampus and frontal cortex, raised [[SLC2A1]] (GLUT-1) and lowered [[GFAP]] immunofluorescence in frontal cortex, with no change in NeuN-stained neurons. Immobilization stress produced endothelial protrusions, more open or damaged tight junctions, a thicker basement membrane and astrocytic oedema in hippocampus.
- Mast cell deficiency, or the stabilizer [[Cromolyn]], lowered stress-induced barrier leak. Mast cell TNF-α is said to down-regulate occludin, claudin-5, [[TJP1]] (ZO-1) and VE-cadherin, and tryptase lowered occludin and claudin-5 in cultured brain endothelial cells. See [[Tight junction]].

**Stress and Alzheimer pathology.**
- In animals, mild to moderate stress raised [[APP]], Aβ, tau phosphorylation ([[MAPT]]), [[Neurofibrillary tangle]] formation and plaques; chronic mild stress in [[APPswe-PS1dE9 mouse]] animals added depressive behaviour, excess Aβ, glial activation and neuroinflammation, and chronic noise stress raised Aβ in young SAMP8 mice. Aβ was reported to rise after only one hour of restraint.
- [[Cortisol]] (corticosterone in rodents) rises with stress and in people with dementia; a 50-year follow-up of about 13,000 people is cited for late-life depression raising dementia risk. Chronic restraint or social defeat lowers [[BDNF]], and early-stage Alzheimer patients show lower pro-BDNF and BDNF.
- Aβ crosses the barrier through LRP-1 and RAGE on endothelium; RAGE-linked Aβ toxicity damages tight junctions, and occludin expression is said to be raised in dementia and Alzheimer disease. Matrix metalloproteinases and [[APOE]] ε4 are named as modifiers of junction integrity.
- Clinically, the mast cell inhibitor [[Masitinib]] as an add-on in mild to moderate Alzheimer disease is reported to have improved cognition in a trial; a physical-activity study in an Alzheimer mouse model is reported to blunt restraint-stress effects.

## Why This Source Matters

It supplies a mechanistic bridge between two topics that otherwise sit apart in this literature: psychosocial stress as a risk factor for dementia, and immune and vascular routes to [[Neuroinflammation]]. Mast cells are the proposed link, which makes them a candidate target (stabilizers such as [[Cromolyn]], tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as [[Masitinib]], CRH receptor blockade) beyond the usual amyloid and tau drugs. The review also explains why amyloid-directed drugs have not been disease modifying in its view, and why barrier junction proteins such as [[CLDN5]] and [[OCLN]] deserve molecular study in Alzheimer tissue.

## Assessment

- This is a narrative mini-review with no stated search method, so selection of cited work is not reproducible; the authors cite their own earlier work on stress and mast cells ("we and others").
- Most direct evidence is from rodents (immobilization, restraint, noise and social stress, and transgenic Alzheimer mice). The authors themselves note that animal stress models transfer poorly to people and that direct human evidence for stress-induced mast cell activation causing Alzheimer disease is missing.
- Several statements are mutually uncomfortable: the text says stress inhibits immune responses yet worsens neuroinflammation, and says some studies find no association between Aβ and barrier dysfunction, without resolving the matter.
- The masitinib trial is mentioned in one sentence, with no design, size or effect, so it cannot be weighed here.
- The figure legends describe pathways (CRH receptor, NK-1, S1P receptors, PAR-2) as established; the text reports most links as associations.

## Open Questions

- Does selective mast cell inhibition or depletion prevent stress-induced amyloid and tau accumulation in an Alzheimer mouse model (evidence so far is correlative and indirect)?
- Which tight junction and adherens junction proteins relocate or are lost in post-mortem Alzheimer brain endothelium, and are the changes the same as those seen after stress in rodents (ultrastructural and molecular data in humans are lacking)?
- Does early-life stress raise late-onset Alzheimer disease risk through lasting changes in mast cell number or activation state (no data exist yet, and dependence on sex or population is also unknown)?
- What were the design, size and effect sizes of the clinical trial of masitinib in mild to moderate Alzheimer disease (given in one sentence, no trial identifier in the text)?
