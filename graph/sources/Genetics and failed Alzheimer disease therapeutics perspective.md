# Genetics and failed Alzheimer disease therapeutics perspective

A single-author perspective from a teacher of graduate neurogenetics, published in 2021 in the journal Genetics. It asks why four decades of work and a run of failed Phase-3 trials have produced no safe and effective disease-slowing drug for [[Alzheimer disease]], and argues that human genetics was misapplied: the rare, dominant, early-onset familial forms were taken as the template for the common late-onset disease, and the [[Amyloid cascade hypothesis]] hardened into dogma.

## How genetics steered the field toward amyloid

- Plaques and tangles: Alzheimer and his contemporaries drew two lesions in autopsy brain. Senile plaques are extracellular deposits of [[Amyloid-beta]] derived from [[APP]]; [[Neurofibrillary tangle]] are intracellular paired helical filaments of hyperphosphorylated tau ([[MAPT]]). Plaque numbers rise with normal ageing and can be high in cognitively normal people, whereas tangle number and distribution track cognitive decline better, yet tangles were left out of the first neuropathology diagnostic criteria.
- The genetic discoveries, in time order: APP mapped to chromosome 21 in 1987, which fits the early amyloid deposition and Alzheimer disease of people with [[Down syndrome]] (too much APP is bad); the first APP mutation family in 1991, then missense alleles, whole-gene duplications and regulatory changes that raise expression (a gain of function); in 1995 dominant mutations in [[PSEN1]] and [[PSEN2]], which encode subunits of gamma-secretase, the enzyme that cuts APP inside the membrane. Mutations in all three genes raise production of amyloidogenic Aβ.
- Tau pointed the other way: dominant [[MAPT]] mutations cause [[Frontotemporal dementia]] with parkinsonism and tau pathology, not Alzheimer disease, so the tau genetics seemed to lead away from Alzheimer disease and the tangles were easy to overlook.
- Two therapeutic strategies followed, both reliant on mouse models: monoclonal antibodies that clear amyloid (names ending in "umab") and inhibitors of [[BACE1]] that reduce its production (names ending in "cestat"). The blood-brain-barrier-penetrant tracer [[Pittsburgh compound B]] ([[Amyloid PET]]) let plaque burden be followed in living trial participants; a comparable tau ligand took more than a decade longer.

## The trial record

- Antibodies: [[Bapineuzumab]] (Johnson and Johnson with Pfizer) failed a Phase-3 trial in mild-to-moderate disease in 2012; [[Solanezumab]] (Eli Lilly) failed in mild disease some years later; [[Crenezumab]] (Roche) failed in two Phase-3 trials in prodromal or mild disease in 2019.
- BACE inhibitors: [[Lanabecestat]] failed for lack of efficacy; [[Verubecestat]] and [[Atabecestat]] failed on serious toxicity concerns.
- Some agents cleared plaque as designed without slowing cognitive decline.
- [[Aducanumab]], an antibody said to target oligomeric Aβ, failed a futility analysis in two Phase-3 trials in 2019 and was dropped, then revived on a reanalysis of the high-dose groups (benefit in only one of the two trials). An independent FDA advisory panel voted against approval in November 2020, yet the agency granted conditional accelerated approval in June 2021 on the amyloid-PET surrogate, with a Phase-4 trial required; three panel members resigned in protest, and several health systems declined to use it. The author frames this as a procedural and scientific controversy and asks whether the agency has, inadvertently, endorsed the amyloid hypothesis.
- Gosuranemab, an antibody against extracellular N-terminal tau fragments, failed all clinical endpoints in a Phase-2 trial in [[Mild cognitive impairment]] or mild disease although it lowered the target in cerebrospinal fluid; see [[Gosuranemab]].
- The best remaining test of the hypothesis is the ongoing prevention trial of crenezumab in carriers of [[PSEN1 p.E280A]] in a large Colombian kindred (NCT01998841), where a wrong antibody specificity could still produce a negative result even if the hypothesis is right for familial disease.

## Four vulnerabilities of the amyloid programmes

1. Mouse models lack face, construct and predictive pharmacological validity; see [[Mouse model validity for Alzheimer disease]].
2. Trial participants were more heterogeneous than assumed; see [[Heterogeneity of late-onset Alzheimer disease]].
3. No validated peripheral biomarker of disease activity existed, perhaps because amyloid PET seemed sufficient; it is unknown whether useful blood or cerebrospinal-fluid markers must be specific to late-onset disease or to a subtype.
4. The most significant: the assumption that common [[Late-onset Alzheimer disease]] arises by the same mechanism, only slower, as the very rare (1% or less) fully penetrant [[Familial Alzheimer disease]] and [[Early-onset Alzheimer disease]], although the two onset ranges are separated by two to three decades and late-onset risk is polygenic and environmental ([[APOE]] genotype, head trauma).

Generic explanations that amyloid proponents give for the failures are also listed (dose and route, insensitive cognitive outcome measures, placebo-response genetics, intervention too late at the stage of mild impairment, antibodies aimed at the wrong amyloid species or size class, vascular effects of Aβ that precede parenchymal deposition).

## Suggested path forward

- A network map of pathogenesis, not a linear pathway, with the cellular events upstream of neuronal death (axonal transport, synaptic function, metabolism, macromolecular synthesis) identified.
- Valid models, defined jointly by geneticists and neuroscientists; patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell lines and higher mammals as parallel routes; genetic model organisms as whole-animal bioassays for a molecule or pathway.
- Tau remains uncertain: which hyperphosphorylated species is pathogenic is unclear, and a recent report proposed that tau oligomers interfere with protein synthesis through methylated RNA transcripts.
- [[Neuroinflammation]] with microglia and astrocytes, which may also explain [[Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities]] (antibody-Aβ complexes activate microglia), and infection, in particular the neurotropic alphaherpesviruses VZV, HSV-1 and HSV-2 because drugs and vaccines already exist.
- Genetics priorities: the mechanism of [[APOE]] genotype effects, the strongest single-gene modifier of late-onset risk, and somatic mutations in brain.
- Near-term practical angles: treating neuronal hyperexcitability with [[Levetiracetam]], and the overlap of [[Cerebral amyloid angiopathy]] and cerebrovascular disease with late-onset disease, where stroke prevention is advocated to lower dementia risk.

## Why This Source Matters

It is a clear statement of the argument that the amyloid-targeted trial failures are partly a consequence of how familial genetics was read: familial mutations proved that amyloid production can cause disease, not that lowering amyloid late in a polygenic, heterogeneous disease will slow it. It also supplies a dated record of the individual drugs, their sponsors and their outcomes up to mid-2021, and it names the model, subtype and biomarker gaps that later studies of animal models, genetic risk and cell-type-resolved expression try to fill.

## Assessment

- An opinion and teaching-derived perspective, not a systematic review: there are no search methods, and the author says the disease terrain is outside their own research expertise.
- The reference citations were stripped in the converted text (many sentences end in "; see Web Resources"), so claims such as the four late-onset subtypes, the tau-PET subtyping and the trial dates cannot be checked against their sources from this document alone.
- Critical statements about the FDA, Biogen and the "cabal" of amyloid proponents are the author's judgement and journalistic and regulatory reporting, not experimental findings.
- The text is dated to mid-2021: the crenezumab prevention trial was still running and the aducanumab Phase-4 results were not yet available.
- The mouse-model critique rests on common-sense validity criteria and a few cited studies, and it is partly offset by the author's own mention of newer models and the MODEL-AD Consortium.

## Open Questions

- Did crenezumab delay symptom onset in cognitively healthy carriers of the PSEN1 E280A mutation in the Colombian kindred prevention trial (NCT01998841; the paper gives only the design and an estimated 2022 completion)?
- Did the required Phase-4 confirmatory trial of aducanumab show clinical benefit or unacceptable amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (the paper predates any result)?
- Do the four late-onset Alzheimer disease subtypes differ in response to anti-amyloid antibodies (the subtype meta-analysis is cited without detail in the paper)?
- Which validity criteria should a mouse or stem-cell model meet before it is used to predict a Phase-3 outcome in late-onset Alzheimer disease (the paper calls for them but does not define them)?
