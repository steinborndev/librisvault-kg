# Gene2Drug repurposing candidates for Alzheimer disease risk genes

A computational study that asks which existing compounds shift the pathways in which [[Alzheimer disease]] risk genes sit. Risk genes come from a large late-onset GWAS and from a single-nucleus atlas of the prefrontal cortex; the compounds are ranked with the [[Gene2Drug]] method and checked against the literature. See [[Drug repurposing]] for the general strategy.

## Design

- Gene lists (972 genes in four lists): 255 late-onset risk genes from a GWAS of 71,800 clinically diagnosed cases and 383,378 controls (136 fine-mapped genes, 192 FUMA-mapped genes and 97 MAGMA genes, merged); and 717 differentially expressed genes comparing early with late pathology in excitatory neurons (532), astrocytes (103) and oligodendrocytes (82), taken from 80,660 single nuclei of 48 individuals and filtered at adjusted p < 0.05 and fold change of at least 1.5. Inhibitory neurons, oligodendrocyte progenitors and microglia were left out because their single-nucleus signal did not agree with bulk RNA-seq. See [[Genome-wide association study]] and [[Single-nucleus RNA sequencing]].
- Function and overlap: [[Gene set enrichment analysis]] of each list in Enrichr, then overlap with the targets of drugs in phase 1, 2 and 3 Alzheimer trials (34, 109 and 49 genes), collected by hand from the Therapeutic Target Database, DrugBank and PubChem.
- Drug ranking: [[Gene2Drug]] over Connectivity Map profiles (five cell lines, 1,309 small molecules) and eight pathway databases, giving 32 drug lists at false discovery rate 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg). Three databases (canonical pathways, microRNA targets, transcription factor targets) gave few drugs and were dropped, leaving 20 lists; drugs were ranked by median enrichment-score rank, then median adjusted-p rank, and the top five per gene list were taken forward (20 drugs).
- Validation: literature review in PubMed, PubChem (toxicity) and ClinicalTrials.gov. Twelve of the 20 were dropped as duplicates or for lack of information, leaving eight.

## Findings

- The GWAS list is enriched for negative regulators of amyloid precursor protein catabolism, among them [[BIN1]], [[ABCA7]], [[APOE]], [[CLU]], [[SORL1]] and [[PICALM]]. Astrocyte genes were enriched for myelin maintenance (CXCR4, SOD1) and axonogenesis (NRP1, FEZ1, S100A6, CXCR4, CCK).
- Five GWAS-list genes are targets of drugs in Alzheimer trials: [[TREM2]], [[CD33]], [[CHRNA2]], [[PRSS8]] and [[ACE]]. Three excitatory-neuron genes are too: [[TKT]], [[APP]] and [[GABRA1]]. The astrocyte and oligodendrocyte lists had no overlap. The overlapping genes were also linked to generalized epilepsy in Enrichr disease terms.
- Eight candidates: [[Ellipticine]], [[Alsterpaullone]], [[Tomelukast]], [[Ginkgolide A]], [[Chrysin]], [[Ouabain]], [[Sulindac sulfide]] and [[Lorglumide]]. Ellipticine, alsterpaullone and tomelukast ranked high for excitatory neurons and also appeared for astrocytes or oligodendrocytes; no drug overlapped between the cell-type lists and the GWAS list.
- Four are judged worth pursuing (alsterpaullone, ginkgolide A, chrysin, ouabain) on preclinical evidence and moderate toxicity: alsterpaullone through inhibition of [[GSK3B]], [[CDK5]] and ERK1/2 and so less tau phosphorylation; ginkgolide A through NMDA receptor and JNK inhibition and PI3K-AKT activation; chrysin through lower NF-κB p65 and C/EBP activity and higher NRF2 antioxidant signalling; ouabain through Na+/K+-ATPase and [[MTOR]] inhibition and activation of [[TFEB]], favouring autophagic clearance.
- The other four are set aside: ellipticine (anticancer, only early apoptosis blocked), tomelukast (a CysLT1 antagonist that also acts on [[PPARG]] and [[PPARA]]; hepatomegaly in rodents and gastrointestinal effects in monkeys), sulindac sulfide (γ-secretase modulation and PPARγ agonism, but gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding) and lorglumide (cholecystokinin antagonist with inconsistent animal results).
- Of the licensed Alzheimer drugs, [[Memantine]] appears in the GWAS, excitatory-neuron and oligodendrocyte drug lists and [[Galantamine]] in the excitatory-neuron list, both significant but not near the top; [[Donepezil]] and rivastigmine are named in the search but not reported as hits.

## Why This Source Matters

It shows how two kinds of genetic evidence, common-variant association and cell-type expression change, can be turned into a ranked list of compounds without a protein-interaction network. The shortlist is a set of hypotheses with mixed quality: several of the four favoured drugs hit tau kinases and neuroinflammation at once, which fits the multi-target idea the authors favour, but none has been tested in an Alzheimer clinical trial.

## Assessment

- Candidates are ranked by transcriptional response in cancer cell lines (Connectivity Map), not neurons, and any compound that moves a pathway counts, so unrelated drugs can be selected; the authors say this needs stricter screening.
- No experimental test of any drug is reported; evidence is the authors' literature reading, and the selection of four out of eight rests on judgement of toxicity and mechanism.
- The text is inconsistent on details: the GWAS case count is given as 71,800 and later 71,880, and the discussion names microglia among the cell types analysed whereas the methods used excitatory neurons, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes.
- Microglia, the cell type with the strongest genetic link to Alzheimer risk, were excluded because of weak agreement with bulk data; only prefrontal cortex was used, not hippocampus.
- The statement that ellipticine derivatives are used for obesity and parkinsonism is made without support in the text.

## Open Questions

- Do the four favoured candidates (alsterpaullone, ginkgolide A, chrysin, ouabain) lower tau phosphorylation or amyloid-beta in human neurons or an Alzheimer mouse model (only literature evidence from other contexts was used)?
- Would a Gene2Drug run on microglial and inhibitory-neuron risk genes, or on hippocampal single-nucleus data, give different candidates (these cell types and region were excluded from the analysis)?
- Can brain-penetrant, low-toxicity forms of chrysin be made, given its poor intestinal absorption and rapid elimination (only a lipid nanoparticle study in rats is cited)?
- Do the memantine and galantamine hits among the GWAS and cell-type drug lists show that the pipeline recovers licensed drugs more often than chance (not tested; ranks were not near the top)?
