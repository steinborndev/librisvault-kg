# Brain-targeted BACE1 siRNA nanomedicine in Alzheimer disease mice

A preclinical study of a gene-silencing nanomedicine for [[Alzheimer disease]]. A cationic dendrimer ([[Polyamidoamine dendrimer]], PAMAM) was PEGylated, decorated with the rabies-virus-derived [[RVG29 peptide]] and loaded with an siRNA against [[BACE1]], giving [[PPR@siBACE1 nanomedicine]]. The aim was to cut amyloid beta production in neurons and, at the same time, restore the amyloid-clearing ability of microglia, by silencing BACE1 in both cell types ([[RNA interference]]). The authors report that, after five intravenous doses in 8-month-old FAD4T mice, the carrier reached the hippocampus, lowered BACE1, cut brain Aβ1-42 by 52%, calmed glial activation and improved memory.

## Design

- Carrier: PAMAM-PEG (PP, non-targeted control) and PAMAM-PEG-RVG29 (PPR). siRNA was loaded by simple mixing; a PAMAM-to-siRNA mass ratio of 5:1 retained negative-control siRNA almost completely on a gel and was used throughout.
- Rationale: RVG29 binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on the blood-brain barrier and on neurons, giving receptor-mediated transcytosis then neuronal uptake; microglia were expected to take up particles by phagocytosis. Neuronal BACE1 drives Aβ production, while microglial BACE1 is linked to loss of the phagocytic DAM-1 state (see [[Disease-associated microglia]]).
- Cell models: [[bEnd.3]] (brain endothelium), [[Neuro2a]] (neurons) and [[BV2]] (microglia), including a Transwell blood-brain barrier model with bEnd.3 on top and N2a or BV-2 below.
- Mice: wild-type C57BL/6J (biodistribution, n = 3 per group) and 8-month-old male [[FAD4T mouse]] (treatment; n = 5 per group of PPR@siBACE1, PP@siBACE1, free siBACE1 or PBS, plus a wild-type PBS group), 5 injections every other day at 10 mg/kg PAMAM and 1000 μg/kg siRNA.
- Readouts: dynamic light scattering, electron microscopy, zeta potential, flow cytometry and confocal imaging of Cy5-labelled siRNA, endocytosis inhibitors, qRT-PCR and Western blot of BACE1, open field, nesting, novel object recognition and Morris water maze, Aβ immunostaining and Aβ1-42 ELISA in brain and in circulating exosomes, Iba1 and GFAP staining, cytokine mRNA, haematoxylin and eosin staining of organs. One-way ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc test.

## Findings

- Particle properties: hydrodynamic size about 100 nm for PPR@siRNA and 70 nm for PP@siRNA, but about 20 nm by electron microscopy (the authors attribute the difference to a hydration layer). Zeta potential reversed from positive to negative on siRNA loading. Stable in PBS for a week; in 20% serum the size rose from day 4 to 5, suggesting protein adsorption over longer incubation.
- Uptake: PPR@siRNA was taken up more than PP@siRNA by bEnd.3 and N2a cells; in BV-2 the two were comparable, and free siRNA was taken up least in all three lines. Cy5 signal overlapped poorly with a lysosome dye, read as lysosomal escape through a proton-sponge effect.
- Uptake route: in bEnd.3, [[Amiloride]] and [[Chlorpromazine]] cut PPR@siRNA uptake by 67.5% and 77.1%; free RVG29 also lowered it. In N2a cells most inhibitors lowered uptake, with [[Genistein]] as strong as chlorpromazine. In BV-2 only chlorpromazine and genistein acted and free RVG29 did nothing, so microglial uptake appears to be nicotinic-receptor independent, probably phagocytic (scavenger, Fcγ or complement receptors). See [[Endocytosis]] and [[Phagocytosis]].
- Barrier crossing in vitro: PPR@siRNA gave the strongest signal in N2a and BV-2 cells in the lower Transwell chamber, while free siRNA hardly crossed the bEnd.3 layer.
- Silencing in cells: PPR@siBACE1 lowered BACE1 mRNA in N2a more than PP@siBACE1 or free siBACE1, and more than the liposome reagent Lipofectamine 2000; in BV-2 it lowered mRNA and protein but slightly less than PP@siBACE1 at the mRNA level. BV-2 cells treated with PPR@siBACE1 took up more FITC-labelled Aβ, although the difference from PP@siBACE1 was not significant.
- Distribution in mice: brain signal of PPR was about 1.8-fold that of PP at 8 h, strongest in hippocampus (dentate gyrus, CA1, CA3). PPR@siRNA co-localised with Nissl-stained neurons and Iba1-positive microglia; PP@siRNA mainly with microglia.
- Behaviour in FAD4T mice: open-field anxiety and movement unchanged between treatments; PPR@siBACE1 raised nest-building scores to wild-type level, restored the novel-object discrimination index towards wild type and shortened escape latency, increased platform crossings and time in the target quadrant in the water maze. Free siBACE1 and PP@siBACE1 did not.
- Molecular effects in FAD4T mice: lower BACE1 mRNA and protein in hippocampus and cortex; [[MBP]] protein, reduced in AD mice, restored to wild-type level; fewer Aβ plaques; Aβ1-42 down 52% in brain and also lower in plasma exosomes; fewer Iba1-positive microglia and GFAP-positive astrocytes; lower [[TNF]], [[IL1B]] and [[IL6]] mRNA in brain (see [[Neuroinflammation]]).
- Safety: no necrosis or apoptosis in heart, liver, spleen, lung or kidney after five doses (haematoxylin and eosin).

## Why This Source Matters

Small-molecule BACE1 inhibitors lowered Aβ in trials without a cognitive gain, and the authors argue that this reflects their inability to clear existing Aβ. This paper tests a different route: knocking down BACE1 expression with a targeted siRNA carrier so that both the production side (neurons) and the clearance side (microglia) are addressed with a single ligand-modified particle. It documents that a short peptide ligand changes brain delivery and efficacy of the same siRNA, which is a delivery lesson for gene therapy in [[Alzheimer disease]] more broadly, and it adds a nanomedicine view of the [[Amyloid-beta formation]] pathway.

## Assessment

- Small groups (n = 5 for behaviour, n = 3 for biochemistry and imaging) and one mouse model at one age; only five doses and a short follow-up, so durability and chronic safety of BACE1 knockdown are not tested. The authors themselves note that chronic BACE1 inhibition failed in several clinical trials and may disturb substrates such as neuregulin-1 and seizure protein 6.
- Microglial targeting is hypothesised, not shown: free RVG29 did not reduce BV-2 uptake, PP@siBACE1 (without ligand) silenced BACE1 in BV-2 as well as or better than PPR@siBACE1, and the Aβ-uptake gain over PP@siBACE1 was not significant. Neuron versus microglia specificity in vivo rests on co-localisation staining alone.
- Safety evidence is histology of five organs in wild-type mice treated with carrier alone; no blood chemistry, immune activation or brain histology for off-target effects is reported in the text read. Cell-type-specific BACE1 knockdown in the brain was not quantified.
- Behaviour in the text is described as "comparable to wild type" for several tests; the exact effect sizes sit in figures not read here. The text also contains an inconsistency (a Western blot comparison names PPR@siBACE1 against itself), and the BV-2 silencing is described at 24 h in one place and after three days in the methods.
- Authors acknowledge that nicotinic receptors fall as neurons are lost and Aβ accumulates, so the targeting may weaken in advanced disease; dosing before neuron loss is proposed.

## Open Questions

- Does PPR@siBACE1 still reach neurons and lower BACE1 in older or more advanced amyloid models, where nicotinic receptor-bearing neurons are lost (only 8-month-old FAD4T mice were treated)?
- Does adding a microglia-specific ligand such as the MG1 peptide to the carrier raise microglial BACE1 silencing and Aβ phagocytosis more than RVG29 alone (suggested in the discussion but not tested)?
- What are the effects of repeated, long-term BACE1 knockdown by this carrier on neuregulin-1 and seizure protein 6 processing, myelination and hippocampal volume (only five doses were given)?
- Which phagocytic receptors mediate uptake of the carrier by microglia (only inferred from the lack of effect of free RVG29)?
