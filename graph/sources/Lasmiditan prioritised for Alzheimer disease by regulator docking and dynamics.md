# Lasmiditan prioritised for Alzheimer disease by regulator docking and dynamics

A purely computational [[Drug repurposing]] study for [[Alzheimer disease]]. Instead of targeting enzymes or receptors, it targets transcriptional regulators whose inferred activity changes in Alzheimer prefrontal cortex, docks 2,200 approved drugs onto them, tests the best complexes by molecular dynamics, and filters the survivors by predicted brain entry. The result is [[Lasmiditan]], a migraine drug, bound to [[ETV6]].

## Design

- Expression data: three public single-nucleus prefrontal cortex datasets, 81 donors in all (42 Alzheimer, 39 control). ROSMAP supplied Brodmann area 10 (24 cases, 24 controls), the Anderson cohort Brodmann area 46 (7 and 8), and the UCI Alzheimer multiome set (11 and 7) served as validation. Nuclei with fewer than 800 UMIs or 500 genes were removed, and neuronal and myeloid lineages were kept. See [[Single-nucleus RNA sequencing]].
- Regulator activity: regulons (a transcription factor plus its top 50 targets) were built with ARACNe-AP on metacells, activity was inferred with metaVIPER, and differentially active regulators ("DERs") were called with Stouffer's method at Benjamini-Hochberg FDR below 0.05 and an absolute association score above 1.5; ChEA3 enrichment gave a second check. See [[Master regulator inference with ARACNe and metaVIPER]].
- Docking: 23 regulators (the 20 DERs plus Notch1, STAT5A and STAT5B, which showed sex-specific signalling) against 2,200 DrugBank approved drugs with AutoDock Vina, structures from the Protein Data Bank or AlphaFold, pockets scored by DoGSiteScorer (druggable above 0.7). Drugs binding APP, ZEB2, PAX6, ETV6 or ST18 at about -7 kcal/mol or better were kept and redocked with DockThor. See [[Molecular docking]].
- Dynamics: 100 ns simulations in NAMD with the CHARMM36m force field at 303.15 K for the top complexes, scored by protein RMSD, solvent-accessible surface area and hydrogen-bond count. See [[Molecular dynamics simulation]].
- Brain entry: logP, logD, polar surface area, molecular weight, logBB (Clark equation) and a P-glycoprotein efflux rule. See [[Blood-brain barrier permeability prediction]].

## Findings

- Twenty regulators were called: myeloid [[MEF2C]], MEF2A, HIVEP3 and [[ETV6]]; neuronal [[APP]], BAZ2B, MYRF, NFIB, [[PAX6]], [[ST18]], [[ZEB2]], SOX10, NKX6-2, CREB5, KCNH8 and ZNF536; and sex-linked [[TCF4]], [[MAFB]], THRB and FOSL2 (with ZEB2 and KCNH8). Neuronal regulators had lower activity in Alzheimer than control, fell with age, with [[Braak staging]] above stage 4 and in APOE e3/e4 and e4/e4 carriers; myeloid regulators moved in mixed directions. APP sat at the centre of the gene-disease network. PAX6 and NFIB were chosen for follow-up partly because of the eye changes reported in Alzheimer disease; NFIB tracked PAX6 in controls and early disease but not late disease.
- Males showed more autophagy-lysosome pathway activity than females and differential Notch and STAT5a/b signalling; THRB, AHR and FOSL2 were higher in male myeloid cells in Brodmann area 46.
- Twelve drug candidates passed the docking filter. Six have been studied in Alzheimer disease (methotrexate, [[Lisuride]], vardenafil, [[Fluspirilene]], sofosbuvir, [[Olaparib]]) and six have not ([[Tolnaftate]], alatrofloxacin, [[Vorapaxar]], [[Lasmiditan]], [[Bictegravir]], atogepant). Redocking with DockThor gave comparable affinities.
- Predicted to cross the blood-brain barrier: tolnaftate, lisuride, vorapaxar, olaparib, lasmiditan and fluspirilene. Tolnaftate and fluspirilene are likely P-glycoprotein substrates and so poor central nervous system candidates; bictegravir, methotrexate, alatrofloxacin, vardenafil and sofosbuvir are too polar.
- Seven drugs went into dynamics on five regulators (ZEB2, APP, PAX6, ETV6, ST18). ETV6 gave the most stable complexes. Protein RMSD, mean hydrogen bonds and logBB for the ETV6 complexes: lasmiditan 2.98 Å, 68.38, -0.29; tolnaftate 3.13 Å, 65.94, 0.71; olaparib 3.25 Å, 71.20, -0.78; fluspirilene 3.26 Å, 68.39, 0.42; bictegravir 3.34 Å, logBB -1.105 (hydrogen-bond count not given); lisuride 4.17 Å, 65.57, -0.19. Vorapaxar with APP had 4.15 Å and only 15.20 hydrogen bonds.
- Lasmiditan is ranked first because it has the lowest RMSD and a high hydrogen-bond count together with logP and polar surface area in the favoured window (logP 3 to 4, TPSA 60 to 75 Å2) and no efflux flag. Lisuride is set aside because of ergot-related cardiac and pulmonary fibrosis risk, hallucinations and absence of US approval. Lasmiditan has no registered Alzheimer trial, but it is a 5-HT1F agonist without vasoconstriction, with dizziness, drowsiness and fatigue as side effects, and no known amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (a risk of [[Anti-amyloid antibody therapy]]).
- ETV6 is proposed as the target because it marks monocytes and microglia, was altered in Brodmann area 10 myeloid cells, and forms stable complexes with several drugs; the authors concede it has hardly been linked to Alzheimer disease before.

## Why This Source Matters

It shows a route from cell-type-resolved human brain expression to a drug shortlist that does not rely on known protein targets, treating transcription factors as ligandable. The conclusion that lasmiditan should be tested is a hypothesis: nothing was measured in a cell, animal or patient, and the binding evidence is simulation of one complex.

## Assessment

- Only in silico evidence; docking scores near -7 kcal/mol and 100 ns simulations of single complexes say little about real affinity, and no binding assay, ETV6 activity readout or cell model is reported. Whether lasmiditan changes ETV6 target-gene expression is unknown.
- The ranking is not clean on the authors' own numbers: olaparib has more hydrogen bonds with ETV6 (71.20) than lasmiditan (68.38), fluspirilene matches lasmiditan within 0.01, and lasmiditan's logBB (-0.29) is lower than three of the other candidates. The claim of "highest H-bond count" is therefore loose, and "optimal" brain entry rests on the logP/TPSA window rather than on the logBB value.
- Olaparib is listed among the drugs with predicted brain entry and bictegravir was carried into dynamics with the same group, yet the discussion reports poor permeability for both (logBB -0.78 and -1.105); the selection step is not fully consistent.
- Pharmacokinetic values are computed from a linear equation and descriptors, not measured; the stated simulation timestep of 2 picoseconds is probably a slip for femtoseconds. Several compounds are misspelled in places (Tonaftate, Olaparin).
- The dataset is small (81 donors, 15 in the Anderson cohort), almost entirely non-Hispanic White where reported, and only prefrontal cortex; the UCI set has no race data and no Brodmann area.
- A drug approved for single acute doses may not be usable chronically; the authors say so.

## Open Questions

- Does lasmiditan bind ETV6 in a biochemical assay such as surface plasmon resonance or thermal shift (only docking and 100 ns simulations were run)?
- Does lasmiditan change ETV6 target-gene expression or inflammatory state in human microglia or iPSC-derived myeloid cells (no wet-lab test was reported)?
- Is ETV6 activity lower or higher in Alzheimer microglia in independent single-nucleus data from hippocampus or entorhinal cortex (only prefrontal cortex was analysed)?
- Would docking the other 19 regulators, or the three signalling proteins, against the same drug library give a different top candidate (only five regulators passed the docking filter)?
- Can lasmiditan reach effective brain concentrations on chronic dosing at a dose that engages ETV6 (the drug is approved only as a single dose for acute migraine)?
