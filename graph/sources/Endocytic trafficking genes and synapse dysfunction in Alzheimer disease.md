# Endocytic trafficking genes and synapse dysfunction in Alzheimer disease

A narrative review of the earliest synaptic failures in [[Alzheimer disease]], organised around endocytic trafficking. It asks what damages synapses before plaques and tangles form, and splits the answer into two halves: mechanisms driven by amyloid beta (Aβ) in early-onset familial disease ([[Familial Alzheimer disease]]), and mechanisms that may act without Aβ through late-onset risk genes that control trafficking ([[Late-onset Alzheimer disease]]). Tau-dependent synaptic damage is left out on purpose, because Aβ42 oligomerisation is said to precede tau aggregation.

## Framing

- Synapse loss, more than plaques or tangles, is the best pathological correlate of cognitive decline (see [[Synapse loss]]), and synapses become dysfunctional before either hallmark appears.
- Familial disease from [[APP]], [[PSEN1]] and [[PSEN2]] mutations is about 1% of patients; the shift of research to late-onset disease follows from that. Heritability of late-onset disease is quoted as 58 to 79%, with [[APOE]] ε4 the strongest risk factor and a meta-analysis of [[Genome-wide association study]] data extending the original "top 10" genes to 37 putative risk factors.
- The authors' Gene Ontology analysis (Cytoscape StringApp) sorts nearly half of the 37 into three pathways: endocytic trafficking, immune response and lipid metabolism, with [[ABCA7]] and [[TREM2]] in more than one. Eleven are trafficking genes; seven are reviewed for a synaptic link: APOE, ABCA7, [[BIN1]], [[CD2AP]], [[PICALM]], [[SORL1]] and [[EPHA1]].
- Presynaptic and postsynaptic background: the [[Synaptic vesicle cycle]] (SNARE fusion, clathrin and AP-2 or AP-3 adaptors, BAR-domain proteins, [[DNM1]] scission) and regulated [[AMPA receptor trafficking]] underlying [[Long-term potentiation]] and depression.

## Aβ-dependent mechanisms (review of the literature)

- Physiological role: picomolar Aβ potentiates transmission and synaptic vesicle recycling, and nanomolar Aβ depresses it; blocking endogenous production potentiates transmission. Aβ may bind [[CHRNA7]] to raise presynaptic calcium. It is unclear whether Aβ40 and Aβ42 differ in this role.
- Intracellular Aβ accumulates in the limiting membrane of late endosomes and multivesicular bodies, pre- and especially postsynaptically, and oligomerises there. In neurons overexpressing APP with the Swedish mutation it comes with loss of [[DLG4]] (PSD-95), AMPA and NMDA receptors and later of [[SYP]] (synaptophysin), so the postsynaptic side appears to fail first. The Osaka mutation reduces spines through disturbed BDNF, mitochondrial and endosomal transport; Aβ also disturbs endosomal sorting of [[NTRK2]] (TrkB) for lysosomal degradation.
- Presynaptic damage: sustained activity reduces vesicle recycling in APP-overexpressing neurons; depletion of [[DNM1]] (dynamin-1) is proposed to underlie slowed endocytosis in familial disease mouse models.
- Extracellular or secreted Aβ acts on the same targets. It can be re-endocytosed, alone or as a complex with ApoE through [[LDLR]] and [[LRP1]], and it can rupture endosomal membranes; in vitro, cytosolic Aβ oligomers bind [[STX1A]] (syntaxin-1a) and block SNARE assembly, and block synaptophysin-VAMP2 complexes, drawing a parallel with alpha-synuclein in [[Parkinson disease]]. Extracellular Aβ raises calcium, drives AMPA and NMDA receptor phosphorylation and removal by endocytosis, and increases degradation of [[EPHB2]], which regulates NMDA receptor currents and memory.
- Aβ may depend on APP at the synapse as its receptor: APP knockout neurons resist exogenous Aβ, and synthetic Aβ no longer reduces PSD-95 when production is blocked.
- Model-specific findings: Aβ dimers from human brain lower spine density and shift plasticity toward depression, with depression needing mGluR5 and spine loss needing NMDA receptors; in APP/PS1 mice postsynaptic loss is minor and presynaptic release fails because Aβ lowers PtdIns(4,5)P2, with rescue by blocking mGluR5-driven PIP2 hydrolysis ([[GRM5]]).

## Late-onset risk genes and synapses (Aβ-independent)

- [[APOE]]: ApoE4 differs from ApoE3 by one amino acid, misfolds, recycles poorly and is hypolipidated. In APOE4 knock-in and APOE knockout mice spine density, neuronal complexity and plasticity fall, and calcineurin ([[PPP3CA]]) activity rises, which favours AMPA receptor removal. ApoE receptors [[LRP8]] (ApoER2) and [[VLDLR]] regulate vesicle release through Reelin ([[RELN]]); ApoE4 traps ApoER2 in postsynaptic recycling endosomes, dampening NMDA receptor activation. [[CLU]] accumulates in synapses of APOE4 carriers' brains. Correcting APOE4 to APOE3 in patient iPSC neurons lowered synaptic density and release without changing secreted Aβ.
- [[ABCA7]]: lipid transporter; knockout mice show altered brain lipids and impaired spatial memory, healthy carriers show impaired cortical connectivity, and a genetic interaction with APOE4 on memory is reported. Direct synaptic mechanisms are not shown.
- [[BIN1]]: expression in Alzheimer brain is contradictory (transcript up, protein down, neuronal isoform down and ubiquitous isoform up). Human BIN1 transgenic mice show early neurodegeneration in the entorhinal cortex-dentate gyrus pathway before plaques. Amphiphysin-1 knockout and Bin1 knockdown impair vesicle recycling and cause seizures; Bin1 knockdown lowers AMPA currents and surface GluA1; it also restrains early-endosome size through [[RIN3]] and Rab5.
- [[CD2AP]]: no direct synaptic data in mammals; the fly homologue Cindr is presynaptic and needed for synaptic maturation and vesicle recycling through a complex with 14-3-3 that controls calcium channel and synapsin levels.
- [[PICALM]]: encodes the clathrin adaptor CALM, paralog of AP180; CALM sorts VAMP2 and synaptic vesicle proteins and modulates GluA2 endocytosis postsynaptically.
- [[EPHA1]]: its synaptic role in Alzheimer disease is unknown; the broader Eph family regulates spines and NMDA receptor recruitment, and [[EPHA4]] activation causes spine retraction.
- [[SORL1]]: reduced in Alzheimer and mild cognitive impairment brain; acts as a sorting factor for TrkB, directs phosphorylated synapsin to calpain-mediated degradation through 14-3-3, and restrains EphA4 activation by ephrinA1, so less SORL1 could drive spine retraction even without Aβ.
- The authors note that direct supporting data are missing for most of these links, and that the genes, like Aβ, converge on the vesicle cycle and glutamate receptor trafficking.

## Why This Source Matters

It gives one place where the amyloid route and the genetic-risk route to synapse failure are laid side by side, and it argues that late-onset risk genes of endocytic trafficking may disturb synapses directly. That frames synaptic vesicle cycling and glutamate receptor endocytosis as the common early targets, and it flags where evidence is thin (CD2AP, EPHA1, ABCA7) so that follow-up work on the risk genes can be prioritised. It also supports the case for models carrying late-onset risk alleles rather than only APP or presenilin mutations.

## Assessment

- A narrative review without a stated search strategy or inclusion criteria; the selection of the seven genes follows the authors' own gene ontology grouping and their research interests, and no quantitative synthesis is done.
- Much of the Aβ evidence comes from APP-overexpressing neurons, exogenous synthetic Aβ and in vitro binding; the authors acknowledge that these may hide mechanisms linked to ageing or late-onset risk factors. The endosomal escape of Aβ to reach cytosolic SNARE proteins is inferred, with in vitro support only.
- For the late-onset genes the authors write that studies of loss of function are few and studies of patient variants fewer, and that direct synaptic data are lacking for most genes.
- Some statements are internally loose (for example a reference to a different gene symbol, EPH1A, in the abstract for EPHA1).

## Open Questions

- Does loss of CD2AP function impair presynaptic vesicle recycling in mammalian neurons as it does for the fly homologue Cindr (only the Drosophila study exists)?
- Do the detrimental synaptic effects of ApoE4 persist when Aβ accumulation is prevented, in neurons carrying other late-onset risk alleles (so far only APOE4-to-APOE3 correction in one patient iPSC line)?
- Does cytosolic Aβ really reach SNARE proteins in living neurons, rather than only binding syntaxin-1a in vitro (endosomal rupture is inferred, not directly shown)?
- Does reduced SORL1 increase EphA4-driven spine retraction in the absence of Aβ in human neurons (shown for overexpression in mice, not for loss of function)?
- How does EPHA1 contribute to synapse dysfunction in late-onset Alzheimer disease (no functional study is cited)?
